Why AFRIP exists

AFRIP — the AI-Powered Flood Recovery & Village Development Intelligence Platform — was built in response to the unprecedented, atypical floods that struck Upper Assam in July 2026. Those floods did not follow the pattern the region plans around.

The problem it addresses is not that relief does not happen. It is that after thirty or sixty days nobody can say which village received what, records of orphaned children go missing, and money that was sanctioned cannot be traced to what it bought. So the platform is built around one rule: records are added and corrected, never quietly overwritten. Every change is attributed to the person who made it and kept.

Start here: the villages you can see are demonstration data

Every village currently on this platform is demonstration data, put there so you can look around. They are labelled Demo wherever they appear, and they are excluded from the real totals. Nothing about them describes a real household.

They sit in Sivasagar, Charaideu and Jorhat — three of the districts affected in July 2026 — so the shape of the data is realistic even though the records are not.

When real villages are registered they carry no such label, and the public summary counts them separately. A count that silently mixed the two would be the exact failure this platform exists to prevent.

Getting an account

  1. Open Operations.
  2. Choose Continue with Google, or Create an account with an email address and a password.
  3. If you signed in with an account that already existed, you will be asked to Register with AFRIP. That is one click, and it is deliberate — see below.

New accounts get the citizen role: read the dashboards, report an issue in your village, sign up as a volunteer. Anything beyond that — the beneficiary register, public money, assigning NGOs — is granted by an administrator. Nobody can raise their own role by filling in a form.

Why registering is a separate step. This platform shares its sign-in service with several unrelated applications, so holding a valid account there does not make you a user of AFRIP. Membership is something you ask for rather than something that happens to you — otherwise signing up to a neighbouring application would quietly enrol you here.

Forgotten your password? Use the link under the sign-in form. If you signed in with Google you have no password to reset — just use Google again.

Four rules the platform enforces, not just displays

  • One village, one lead NGO. Two organisations cannot both be accountable for the same place, because when both are, neither is.
  • Duplicate aid is flagged, never dropped. If the same household appears to have received the same thing twice, both records survive and the conflict is raised. The platform does not decide which one was a mistake.
  • Spent ≤ released ≤ sanctioned. Arithmetic that cannot be violated, shown as three bars so anyone can check it.
  • A corruption report never routes to the NGO it concerns.

Your data, and other people's

The beneficiary register holds the names and circumstances of people in a disaster — widows, orphaned children, households that have lost everything. It is visible only to district officers and NGO coordinators, never on the public view, and never to a citizen account.

Every change made through this platform is recorded with the account that made it and kept permanently. That applies to you as much as to anyone: the audit trail is what makes the promise above worth anything, and it cannot be edited by the application, including by an administrator.

Where the records physically live is stated at the bottom of every page.

Roughly: a citizen can do 14 things, a village committee member 28, an NGO coordinator 40, and a district officer everything except the two admin-only rows.

One thing worth knowing about ownership. The table is about your role, but you may also edit what you created — a citizen who reports an issue can carry that issue through to resolution, even though the row above says otherwise for issues in general. Rights come from your role or from having made the record.

An administrator is not a role with more permissions — it is a role that is not checked. Admin appears in none of the rules above; the platform simply does not consult them for an administrator. That is why the list of administrators is kept in version control rather than in a settings page, and why there is deliberately only one today.

Found a bug, or want something changed?

Press Report a problem in the header — it is there on every tab once you are signed in. Or go straight to this page's report form, which is the link to share with anybody else:

Your report automatically records which version of the app you were using and which screen you were on, so nobody has to remember. You need an account, because a report nobody can reply to is a report that goes nowhere — if you follow the link signed out, it will ask you to sign in and then open the form for you.

Please do not type anyone's name, phone number or address into a report. Refer to a village, a beneficiary or an issue by its identifier — the code shown on the record, such as village-1. Reports are free text and are read by administrators; identifiers are enough for us to find the record.

People and their roles

Everyone registered with AFRIP. Change somebody's role to give them access to more of the platform — a district officer can already use every workflow, so that is usually the role to give, not administrator.

An administrator is not checked. They can read the beneficiary register, the audit trail and everyone's reported bugs. Appoint one only when somebody needs exactly that. An administrator you appoint here cannot appoint anyone else — that stays with the accounts named in the platform's source code, so this cannot get away from you.

The one line is all there is here. The full description and who asked are deliberately not shown — feature requests are written in free text and can name people, so only administrators see the rest. Bug reports are not listed at all.

Where your data is stored
Records are held in a Supabase PostgreSQL database in AWS eu-west-1 (Ireland). The application itself runs on Google Cloud Run in europe-west2 (London). Personal data therefore rests in the European Union and in the United Kingdom, not in India.
